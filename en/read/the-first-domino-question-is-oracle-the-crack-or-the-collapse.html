The First Domino Question: Is Oracle the Crack, or the Collapse?

By VOSB Desk17 October 2026 at 3:08 pm3 min read

Oracle now carries somewhere between $122 billion and $167 billion in debt, depending on which measure and which quarter you pick. Its credit rating sits one notch above junk. Its free cash flow, which was comfortably positive as recently as early 2025, has gone deeply negative. On the other side of Oracle’s biggest contract sits OpenAI, which has publicly committed $300 billion to buying Oracle’s computing capacity over the next five years but carries almost none of the debt behind it. Both facts are true. Neither is improper. But together they force a question worth answering carefully rather than rhetorically: if Oracle is holding the obligation for capacity that OpenAI merely promised to pay for later, is Oracle actually the first domino in an AI infrastructure debt unwind — or is that the wrong way to think about what’s happening?

Well, Oracle is unlikely to be the first company in this cycle to default or restructure. It is, however, already functioning as the market’s chosen pressure gauge for the entire AI capex story, and has been for the better part of a year. Those are two different claims, and most of the current commentary on Oracle collapses them into one — which is where the panic, and the dismissiveness, both go wrong.

How Oracle got here

Thirty years of enterprise software-licensing built Oracle into a machine that printed cash at seventy-percent margins. Then it lost the first cloud war to Amazon, Microsoft, and Google, and spent much of the 2010s being discussed in the past tense. The AI buildout gave Oracle a second act, and it took it aggressively: total debt grew from roughly $80.5 billion in fiscal 2024 to $100 billion in fiscal 2025 and $122.3 billion by the end of fiscal 2026, with some measures — including total liabilities and mid-2026 quarterly snapshots, running as high as $167 billion.

Capital expenditure hit $55.7 billion in fiscal 2026 alone, overshooting Oracle’s own guidance, and free cash flow for the year came in at negative $23.7 billion even as operating cash flow hit a record $32 billion.

The gap between those two numbers is the entire story: Oracle is generating plenty of cash from its existing business, and spending all of it, plus tens of billions more, building capacity for a business that doesn’t exist yet.

In July 2026, S&P Global responded by cutting Oracle’s long-term issuer rating to BBB-, one notch above speculative grade, citing the scale and pace of that spending.

The customer side of the ledger looks nothing like Oracle’s. OpenAI’s $300 billion, five-year compute commitment, the anchor of the broader Stargate build-out — is a purchase contract, not a loan. OpenAI’s own balance sheet holds over $73 billion in cash and a revolving credit facility of roughly $4.7 billion; it hasn’t borrowed to fund the commitment itself. What it has done is burn cash at a startling rate: $3.7 billion in a single quarter against $5.7 billion of revenue, a $21.3 billion net loss in that same quarter, and no profitable year in its history.

So, the mechanics behind the original question hold up: Oracle borrowed the money to build the data centers. OpenAI made the promise to eventually pay for using them.
